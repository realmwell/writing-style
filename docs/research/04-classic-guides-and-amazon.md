# Classic writing guides and Amazon culture

Access date: 2026-09-12. All sources public web pages, fetched or searched on that date.

## 1. George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946)

Source: https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/

1. Never use a metaphor, simile, or figure of speech you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if an everyday English equivalent exists.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.
7. Dying metaphors (like "toe the line") have lost their force and are used only to save the writer the trouble of inventing a fresh phrase.
8. Verbal false limbs pad sentences by replacing a plain verb with a phrase, turning "break" into "render inoperative."
9. Pretentious diction (words like "phenomenon," "eliminate") dresses up simple statements and gives biased judgments a false air of scientific impartiality.
10. Meaningless words (like "democracy," "fascism") have no agreed meaning and get used dishonestly, to hide what the speaker actually thinks.

## 2. Strunk, The Elements of Style (1918)

Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37134/37134-h/37134-h.htm

1. Make the paragraph the unit of composition: one paragraph to a topic.
2. Open each paragraph with a topic sentence, and close in agreement with it.
3. Use the active voice: "usually more direct and vigorous than the passive."
4. Put statements in positive form; don't rely on negation to make your point.
5. Use definite, specific, concrete language: "prefer the specific to the general, the definite to the vague."
6. Omit needless words: "a sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences."
7. Avoid a string of loose sentences joined by conjunctions.
8. Express coordinate ideas in parallel form.
9. Keep related words together so the connection between them is clear.
10. Keep one tense in a summary; use present tense for dramatic narrative.
11. Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end.
12. Words and expressions commonly misused, most cited: "like" governs nouns and pronouns, not clauses (use "as"); "data" is plural; "due to" belongs after a form of "to be," not as a loose connective; "however" at the head of a sentence should mean "in whatever way," not "nevertheless"; "case," "nature," and "character" are usually padding and can be deleted; avoid the split infinitive when a natural alternative exists; check "whom" against its grammatical role before using it.

## 3. William Zinsser, On Writing Well

Not public domain; drawn from reputable summaries and quoted passages.
Sources: https://www.nateliason.com/notes/writing-well-william-zinsser, https://fuzzynotes.adarshbadri.me/p/william-zinsser-on-writing-well, https://tryathens.com/blog/on-writing-well-summary

1. The four commandments of good writing are clarity, simplicity, brevity, and humanity.
2. "Clutter is the disease of American writing... we are strangling in unnecessary words, circular constructions, pompous frills, and meaningless jargon."
3. Most first drafts can be cut by half without losing anything the reader needs.
4. "The secret of good writing is to strip every sentence to its cleanest components."
5. Complicated subjects call for simpler sentences, not more complicated ones: complexity belongs in the idea, not the expression.
6. "There is no style store; style is organic to the person doing the writing"; the fundamental rule is be yourself.
7. Write for yourself, not for an imagined mass audience: "if you write for yourself, you'll reach the people you want to write for."
8. Unity holds a piece together: one pronoun, one tense, one mood, sustained throughout.
9. "Clear thinking becomes clear writing; one can't exist without the other."
10. A good ending should take the reader slightly by surprise and yet feel exactly right.

## 4. Amazon's writing culture

Sources: https://medium.com/@apappascs/write-like-an-amazonian-14-tips-for-clear-and-persuasive-communication-e2a11afc7362, https://www.sixpagermemo.com/blog/how-to-write-a-six-pager, https://nbm4.com/articles/how-to-amazon-style-six-page-narratives/, https://www.forbes.com/councils/forbescommunicationscouncil/2022/08/30/why-and-how-every-company-should-use-amazons-six-page-memo-format/

1. Meetings open with a silent read of a six-page narrative memo, not a slide deck; Amazon does not use PowerPoint for internal decisions.
2. Bullet points hide fuzzy thinking; full sentences and paragraphs force it into the open.
3. Every proposal must survive the "so what" test: a reader should be able to say what decision the document is asking for and why it matters.
4. Use subject-verb-object sentences.
5. Keep sentences under roughly 30 words.
6. Replace adjectives with data: "significantly better" is not information, a number is.
7. Avoid weasel words and unsupported qualifiers ("would help," "might bring clarity," "arguably the best").
8. Avoid jargon and acronyms; when a technical term is unavoidable, define it on first use.
9. The reader should never have to re-read a sentence to understand it the first time.
10. The memo should anticipate objections and include an FAQ section addressing them directly.
11. Bezos's shareholder letters model the same house style at scale: plain declarative sentences, concrete numbers, and a customer-first frame, reused every year including the standing appendix of the 1997 letter.

## 5. The Economist Style Guide, HBR, and Josh Bernoff

Sources: https://cdn.static-economist.com/sites/default/files/pdfs/style_guide_12.pdf, https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2002/english-economist-style/, https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.psu.edu/dist/d/7249/files/2015/08/HBS-Guide-to-Better-Business-Writing-2005.pdf, https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/28448362-writing-without-bullshit

**Economist Style Guide**
1. "Clarity of writing usually follows clarity of thought. So think what you want to say, then say it as simply as possible."
2. The guide opens by endorsing Orwell's six rules outright.
3. Quotes Orwell's four (or six) questions a scrupulous writer asks: what am I trying to say, what words will express it, what image makes it clearer, is that image fresh, and could I say it more shortly.
4. Cut redundant compounds: "the business environment" becomes "conditions for business."
5. Avoid journalese, slang, and needless Americanisms ("meet with" becomes "meet").

**HBR / Bryan Garner, HBR Guide to Better Business Writing**
6. Summarize key specifics up front; don't bury the point.
7. Cut throat-clearing phrases: "there are," "in order to," "the fact that," "there is no doubt that."
8. Read a draft aloud; if you run out of breath mid-sentence, the sentence is too long.
9. Shorter sentences force clearer thinking, not just clearer prose.

**Josh Bernoff, Writing Without Bullshit**
10. The Iron Imperative: treat the reader's time as more valuable than your own.
11. Purge jargon, weasel words ("many," "most," unsupported qualifiers), and passive voice.
12. Front-load writing: put the point in the title, the subject line, and the first sentence.

## 6. Paul Graham, "Write Simply" and "Write Like You Talk"

Sources: http://paulgraham.com/simply.html, http://paulgraham.com/talk.html

1. Fancy writing can hide the absence of ideas; writing simply keeps you honest, because if you have nothing to say it will be obvious.
2. "The main reason I write simply is that it offends me not to."
3. Editing a draft toward its final form mostly means making it simpler.
4. "Here's a simple trick for getting more people to read what you write: write in spoken language."
5. After a first draft, check each sentence against how you'd say it to a friend, and rewrite it that way if it doesn't match.
6. Read the piece aloud before publishing and fix anything that doesn't sound like conversation, including anything phonetically awkward.

## Consolidated top 25 (rules appearing in two or more sources)

1. Cut every unnecessary word. (Orwell, Strunk, Zinsser, Economist, HBR, Bernoff)
2. Prefer the active voice to the passive. (Orwell, Strunk, Economist, Bernoff, Amazon)
3. Use short, plain words over long or fancy ones. (Orwell, Economist, Graham, Zinsser)
4. Avoid jargon and technical terms when an everyday word works. (Orwell, Economist, Amazon, Bernoff)
5. Prefer concrete, specific, data-backed language over vague qualifiers. (Strunk, Amazon, Bernoff, Economist)
6. Clear thinking produces clear writing; muddled prose reveals muddled thought. (Orwell, Economist, Zinsser)
7. Avoid stale, worn-out phrases and clichés. (Orwell, Economist)
8. Keep sentences short. (Amazon, HBR, Bernoff)
9. Read your draft aloud and fix what sounds wrong. (Graham, HBR)
10. Simplicity is a discipline applied in editing, not a lack of effort. (Zinsser, Graham)
11. Write like you'd speak to another person. (Graham, Zinsser)
12. Avoid weasel words and unsupported hedges. (Amazon, Bernoff)
13. Put statements in positive, definite form rather than piling up negatives. (Strunk, Amazon)
14. Keep related words and ideas together for clarity. (Strunk, HBR)
15. Front-load the point; don't bury the lead. (Bernoff, HBR, Amazon)
16. The reader's attention and time are the writer's first constraint. (Bernoff, Amazon, Zinsser)
17. Avoid padded verb phrases that replace one plain verb with several words. (Orwell, HBR)
18. Maintain one consistent tense and voice throughout a piece. (Strunk, Zinsser)
19. Elaborate diction is often used to disguise thin or dishonest thinking. (Orwell, Graham)
20. Most first drafts are too long; cutting is the main work of revision. (Zinsser, Graham, Orwell)
21. Break a rule before writing something absurd or unnatural in order to follow it. (Orwell, Graham)
22. Full sentences expose weak logic that bullet points can hide. (Amazon, Strunk)
23. Anticipate the reader's objections and answer them in the text. (Amazon, HBR)
24. Avoid Latinate or bureaucratic phrasing when a native word exists. (Orwell, Economist)
25. Style is inseparable from how clearly you think, not decoration on top. (Zinsser, Economist, Orwell)

## Conflicts between sources

- Contractions and register: Strunk's era assumes formal register and gives no endorsement of conversational contractions, while Zinsser and Graham push toward spoken, contraction-friendly prose ("write like you talk"). Strict Strunk reads stiffer than Zinsser or Graham.
- Rules as law versus guidance: Orwell ends his list by telling writers to break every prior rule rather than write something barbarous. Amazon's guidance and the Economist Style Guide read more like fixed house rules (30-word caps, banned phrases) with less room for exception.
- Data over adjectives versus warmth: Amazon's "replace adjectives with data" pushes toward flat, evidence-only prose. Zinsser's humanity and personal voice pull the other way; warmth often lives in the adjectives and asides Amazon's style would cut.
- Bullet points: Amazon treats them as a way to hide weak thinking and bans them from core documents. Most modern business-writing advice, including the anti-AI-tells literature, treats bullets as a legitimate tool for scannability.
- Brevity as hard cap versus byproduct: Amazon and Bernoff set near-mechanical limits. Zinsser treats brevity as something that falls out of clear thinking, not a rule to check against.

## What these guides say that the anti-AI-tells literature doesn't

- Warmth and personality. Zinsser's fourth commandment, humanity, has no equivalent in checklists built around banning em dashes or rule-of-three constructions. Anti-AI-tells guidance flags what to remove; Zinsser asks what personal presence to add back in.
- The writer's own stake in the subject. Zinsser and Graham both write from the premise that you write best about what interests you, and that the reader can feel a writer's genuine engagement or its absence. Detection-driven editing has no concept of authorial interest at all.
- Endings. None of the anti-AI-tells material addresses how to close a piece. Zinsser treats the ending as a deliberate craft decision, arriving slightly ahead of the reader's expectation while still feeling inevitable.
- Unity of tense, pronoun, and mood across a whole piece. AI-tells checklists work sentence by sentence or paragraph by paragraph. Zinsser and Strunk both care about consistency held across an entire document, which is a structural property, not a local one.
- Writing as a proxy for thinking. Orwell, the Economist, and Amazon all treat unclear prose as a symptom of unclear or dishonest thought, not a style defect to patch. That reframes editing as an argument-quality check, which sits above anything a pattern-matching tells list can catch.
- A stated audience relationship. Zinsser's "who am I writing for" and Bernoff's Iron Imperative both put a specific human reader at the center of every choice. Tells-focused editing optimizes against a detector or a pattern list, not a reader.
