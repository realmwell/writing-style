# Clarity rules

## Applies to

Professional, sales outreach, and marketing. Off in personal writing, where Max's habits win. Sources: `docs/research/01-google-style-guide.md` (Google developer documentation style guide) and `docs/research/04-classic-guides-and-amazon.md` (Orwell, Strunk, Zinsser, Amazon, Economist, Bernoff, Graham).

## Sentence rules

Address the reader as "you." Say "I" when Max is the actor. Avoid "we" unless a real group is meant.
Present tense by default. Save "will" for things that happen later.
Active voice. The reader should always know who did what. "FHFA published the bulletin," not "the bulletin was published."
Condition before instruction: "If the ATO lapses, call me," not "Call me if the ATO lapses."
Lead with the point. The first sentence of a paragraph carries its main claim.
Keep sentences short. Under 30 words unless the length is doing work. Read one aloud; if you run out of breath, split it.
Use contractions: don't, isn't, there's, I'd. They read as speech and a negative contraction is harder to misread than "not."
Plain word over fancy word: use, not utilize; start, not commence; help, not facilitate; show, not elucidate; look at, not delve into.
Cut "just," "simply," "easily," "quickly." They add nothing and can read as condescending.
"Lets you," not "allows you to." "Click," not "click on."
Spell out an abbreviation the first time unless the reader uses it daily. Write "for example" and "that is," not "e.g." and "i.e."
Put statements in positive form. "He usually came late," not "he was not very often on time."
Keep related words together. The subject and its verb should not be separated by a clause.
Place the emphatic word at the end of the sentence.

## Paragraph and document rules

One idea per paragraph. Open with the topic sentence.
Two to four sentences per paragraph in email and copy. Let one paragraph be a single line when it earns it.
Sentence-case headings, never title case.
Lists only for a real sequence (numbered) or a real set of comparable items (bulleted). Keep list items grammatically parallel. Everything else is prose.
Descriptive link text. Never "click here."
No heading that holds only other headings.
Skip idioms and culture-bound references when the reader might not share them. In personal writing this rule is off.

## Business rules

Replace adjectives with data. "Significantly better" is not information; a number is. "Cut the run from nine days to two."
No weasel words: "would help," "might bring clarity," "arguably," "many," "most" without a number. <!-- lint:ignore -->
No jargon without a reason. When a technical term is unavoidable, define it on first use.
The reader should never have to reread a sentence.
Front-load. Put the point in the subject line, the first sentence, and the first line of each paragraph.
Anticipate the reader's first objection and answer it in the text, without staging a debate ("here's where I'd push back" is banned).
Treat the reader's time as worth more than your own.
The "so what" test is a useful heuristic, not a rule: if a sentence would not change what the reader thinks or does, cut it.

## Humanity rules

Write like you talk. After drafting, check each sentence against how Max would say it to a colleague, and rewrite it that way.
Read it aloud before delivering. Fix anything that doesn't sound like speech, including anything phonetically awkward.
Keep one tense, one person, one mood across the piece.
Have a stake in the subject. The reader can tell when the writer cares. Say what Max thinks.
End on purpose. The last sentence is the last real point, not a summary and not a flourish. A good ending arrives a beat before the reader expects it.
Simplicity is a discipline applied in editing. Most first drafts can lose a third of their words without losing anything the reader needs.
Break any of these rules before writing something stiff or false to follow it.

## Where clarity yields to voice

The Google guide flattens tone for an anonymous global readership. Max writes to people he can name. Resolve the conflicts by register.

Exclamation points. Google says avoid. Max uses them. Allowed in personal freely, one per email in professional, never in sales or marketing headlines.
Idioms and cultural references. Google forbids for translatability. Allowed in personal and light in professional when the reader will get it. Off in sales.
Humor. Google warns against it. On in personal, light in professional, off in sales and marketing unless the brand calls for it.
"Please." Google limits it to formal requests. Max says "please" in ordinary asks ("Could you please help us arrange that?"). Keep it.
One universal register. Google wants one voice. This skill has three. The register file wins.
Hypotheticals and "would." Google prefers flat present tense. Max hedges through structure ("from the outside it reads as..."); that stays in sales, where the recipient is invited to correct the read.
Figurative language. Google cuts metaphor. Max uses a fresh image now and then. Allowed when the image is his, never a stock one (Orwell's rule).
Heading case. Sentence case everywhere. No exception.
