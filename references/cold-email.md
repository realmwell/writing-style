# Cold email

## When this loads

Any sales outreach: a cold or warm email to a prospect at a named account, a follow-up sequence, or a LinkedIn note that pairs with an email. Sources: `docs/research/05-cold-email-craft.md` and Max's own outreach method for federal civilian, tribal, and regulated-industry accounts. Everything in `references/clarity-rules.md` and `references/anti-ai-patterns.md` applies at full strength.

## Before drafting

Required: the recipient (name, title, office), the account, and account context (what is pressing on this person's desk, with named and dated artifacts). Also the sender line Max wants ("I'm Max Greenberg, the Databricks Enterprise Account Executive covering X").
Optional: an angle Max prefers (risk reduction, found money, efficiency, peer benchmark, velocity) and a financial hook (a dollar figure or a benchmark percentage).
If the recipient or the account context is missing, stop and ask. Do not draft a generic email and hope.
Restate what you have in one line before drafting: account, recipient, sender, angle, hook present or not.

Research first. The strongest first line references something the recipient has personally signaled: a filing, an OIG or GAO finding with its number and date, an open requisition naming a technology, a talk, an appointment release, a budget line. Look for two things: one fact that proves the work was done, and confirmation that this problem sits on this person's desk and not a peer's. Tenure matters: someone in seat under a year moves on a different timeline than someone with five.

## Subject lines

One to five words. Under four words got the highest reply rate in Gong's 85 million email set; Belkins' 5.5 million puts the peak at two to four.
Name an artifact, a number, or a problem. Never a clever line, a question, or a curiosity gap; those raise opens and cut replies.
Lowercase reads as a person, not a blast. Vendor data, moderate evidence; use it unless the artifact is a proper noun.
No exclamation point, no emoji, no "quick question," no "following up," no "touching base," no fake "re:" or "fwd:".
No selling words: demo, solution, opportunity, free, save, unlock. <!-- lint:ignore -->
When Max supplies a dollar figure or a percentage, the subject can carry it.

Examples with placeholder artifacts:

OIG-2025-XX-041 repeat finding
GAO-25-107311, page 12
FY26 request: $4.2M consolidation line
your senior MLflow engineer posting
after the March consent order
tribal broadband RFP closes Friday

## Touch 1 structure

Max's five-paragraph shape, adapted to the data. Fifty to 125 words by default. Up to 200 when Max asks for the full version. Plain text. No attachment, no image, no link unless Max asks (see hard rules).

1. Introduction and observation. "Hi Name, I'm Max Greenberg, the Databricks Enterprise Account Executive covering Account." Then the observation: "In coming up to speed on the account, two things kept surfacing around your function: [dated artifact] and [recipient-specific signal]." One sharp artifact beats two forced ones. Never "recently joined." Never "hope you're well" here.
2. Hypothesis. What those artifacts together imply about the recipient's daily reality, written as a guess the recipient can correct: "From the outside the pattern reads as..." "If that's roughly right, the hard part is probably..." End on the operational consequence in the recipient's own vocabulary. Never describe the recipient's job back to them. Never "given where your seat sits," "at the intersection of," "lands on your desk," "in your wheelhouse."
3. What good looks like. In plain terms, shaped by the angle. Risk reduction: the evidence package the examiner sees. Velocity: the release cadence. Found money: the recovered budget line. Efficiency: the metric that moves. Peer benchmark: what the peer already runs. Name the capability in plain language, not the product name.
4. One customer example. "The closest version of this I've seen run end to end was at Customer," then one qualifier line on why the peer fits ("same regulator, same wholesale model"). Sourced from a Databricks-published customer page, blog post, or summit session, verified live. Named in text; the link goes in the reply or in touch 3.
5. The ask. Default: an interest question. "Worth a look at how they sequenced it?" Gong's 304,000-email CTA study found interest asks beat meeting-time asks about two to one on first touch. Named alternative, when Max asks for it: "Would be great to meet for 30 minutes to compare notes on how a couple of peer teams are sequencing this. Happy to come to you, on your calendar. Appreciate your time, hope to connect soon." No signature block; Max appends it.

Meeting length when the structured close is used: 30 minutes for technology and program leads, 45 for risk and modeling leads.

## The paragraph-two test

Read paragraph two aloud. If the recipient's only honest reply is "yes, that's literally my job description," rewrite it. It passes when the recipient could plausibly reply "actually the harder part is X," and that reply would move the conversation. This test is not optional.

## Openers

Lead with a specific, dated, checkable observation, never a compliment or an industry generality.
Say what you noticed, not that you noticed it. "Your Q3 filing shows X," not "I was reading your Q3 filing and noticed X."
One observation, not three. Three reads as a dossier.
Tie it to a role-level stake, not a personal one. Their audit cycle, yes. Their alma mater, no.

Examples:

"OIG-2025-XX-041 named data reconciliation delays as a repeat finding at three regional offices."
"Your FY26 request lists $4.2M for legacy consolidation, due to OMB in December."
"The senior compliance analyst posting your team opened last week usually means an exam finding is driving headcount."
"The March consent order sets a 180-day remediation window for transaction monitoring, and that clock is close."

## CTAs

One ask per email. Interest, not time, on the first touch. Low cost: a yes or no the reader can give in five seconds. No pressure language; regulated buyers are trained to distrust urgency.

Examples:

"Worth a look at how a peer agency handled this?"
"Want the two-page summary, no call needed?"
"Is this worth a short conversation, or is it not a priority this quarter?"
"Should I send the summary to you, or is this better for someone on your team?"

## Touches 2 to 4

Four touches over about three weeks: day 1, day 5, day 12, day 19. Two to four days apart early, a week or more later.
Touch 2 (75 to 125 words): one new fact or signal since touch 1. Brief.
Touch 3 (125 to 175 words): value, not a pitch. A useful frame or a third-party example. The customer link can go here.
Touch 4 (50 to 100 words): a respectful close that leaves the door open. "I'll close the loop here" gets replies from people who were interested but never answered.
Every follow-up adds something. A bare "bumping this" is the weakest performer in every dataset that measures it.
Reference the earlier email in a clause, then move on. Don't re-pitch.
Align to the buyer's calendar: fiscal year end, appropriations, budget cycles.

## Government and regulated buyers

Show you know the buying mechanics when relevant: contract vehicle, NAICS, set-aside, ATO, FedRAMP level. Omitting them reads as a commercial pitch misapplied.
FedRAMP is a credential, not a hook. Lead with the problem; cite the authorization as proof.
Risk and compliance officers talk like they talk to examiners: findings, remediation windows, audit-ready evidence, the specific regulator (OCC, FDIC, state DOI, SEC, FHFA). Mirror that.
No hype. The SEC has brought AI-washing enforcement actions; a compliance officer connects that dot fast.
Tone: formal but not stiff. Full sentences, no slang, no forced casualness, no jargon stacking.
Sales cycles run six to eighteen months. Nothing in the tone or ask implies an urgency the buyer doesn't feel.

## Hard rules

Do not lie. Do not claim experience with a customer or a use case Max does not have. The customer example comes from Databricks-published material.
Do not manufacture data. No invented dollar figures, contract numbers, bulletin numbers, OIG or GAO IDs, or named people. If a number is unverified, leave it out. A weaker email beats a wrong fact.
Specific over generic. "The recent OIG report" is not an artifact. "OIG-2025-XX-041, February 2025" is.
The customer source is verified live even when it is not linked: fetch it, confirm it returns 200, confirm it is not a redirect to a hub page, confirm the page discusses what you claim.
No link in touch 1 unless Max asks. No attachment, no image.
No flattery opener. No "I hope this finds you well." No "I wanted to reach out." No "I came across your profile."
No exclamation points. No emoji.
Touch 1 is 50 to 125 words by default.
The paragraph-two test is not optional.

## Deliverability words to avoid

free, guarantee, act now, risk-free, no obligation, buy now, winner, 100% satisfied, limited time. <!-- lint:ignore --> Also ALL CAPS words, multiple links, heavy formatting, and any subject that misleads about the body. Wording is a secondary lever; sender authentication and list quality matter more, and those are Max's to manage.

## LinkedIn follow-up

Under 300 characters including spaces. First clause signals it follows the email ("Just emailed you on..."). One sentence on the topic and the artifact. A short ask that mirrors the email. No link if it pushes past 300.

"Hi Name, Max from Databricks. Just emailed you on the OIG-2025-XX-041 repeat finding and what a peer agency did about the reconciliation gap. Worth a look? Appreciate your time."
